# Heat-pump sizing factor measured from HEAPO (stage 1: measurement only)

Generated by `swiss/experiments/hp_sizing_ratio.py report`. No model parameter is changed. The current rule (TR-009, A-024) sizes the electrical nominal power as 1.1 x the highest hourly heat-pump electricity. Here: ratio = (rated heating capacity / norm-point COP) / highest hourly heat-pump electricity in a full meter year near the visit year.

Protocols: 410 visits, 193 of them without a Household_ID (cannot be joined to meter data; 186 of those have a capacity), the rest 214 households; 391 have a heating capacity; 71 have a norm-point COP (the others use the median COP by installation type and norm point, then by type). Meter year: calendar year with at least 90 % complete hours, nearest to the visit year; one protocol per household (latest visit with a capacity).

## Sanity check: SFH-low (HEAPO household 7069411)

Capacity 12.0 kW thermal, norm-point COP 3.30 (protocol), rated electrical input 3.64 kW, highest hourly heat-pump electricity 4.57 kWh in 2022 (visit 2022), ratio 0.80.

## Limitations

- The sample is single-family houses (HEAPO); the ratio would also be applied to the apartment buildings, whose heat pumps and meters differ.
- The heat-pump meter includes hot water where the heat pump also serves it; the dataset builder sizes the space-heating device on space-heating electricity only. The "DHW by heat pump: no" rows compare like with like.
- Rated input is estimated from the thermal capacity and a norm-point COP, not read from a nameplate input power; the norm point differs by type (A2/W35, B0/W35).
- The highest hourly value is one hour of one year; the sizing rule uses the same statistic.
- Small sample: 14 households after exclusions, 11 of them with a fallback COP.
- A ratio below one means the estimated rated input (thermal capacity over the norm-point COP, usually at A2/W35 or B0/W35) is below the highest hourly electricity actually drawn: at colder outdoor air or higher supply temperature the COP is lower, and the meter also records any electric back-up heater, defrost and hot water. The ratio is therefore not a margin over delivered electricity; used as a cap it would saturate the model heat pump far more often than today (see the stage 2 note).

## Heat output (thermal) comparison: model against HEAPO (validation check only; nothing is changed)

A nameplate rating is thermal at a norm point, while the model caps electrical input at any COP, so the comparison is made in heat. Model capacity = nominal electrical power x the model COP (`swiss/models/heat_pump.py`, identical to the CityLearn `HeatPump.get_cop`) at the stated outdoor and supply temperature; nominal power, efficiency and supply temperature are those of the built dataset (`swiss/datasets/climate/hist_none/schema.json`). Design hour = the coldest hour of the 2023 weather file (-12.9 C). Peak heat delivered = highest hour of the thermostat rollout (`heat_peaks.json`); peak heat demand (derived) = highest hour of the heat demand derived from the measured meters (`data/canonical/thermal`).

### Conclusion

The model single-family heat pumps have a rated thermal capacity of 1.50 times their peak delivered heat; the matched real houses have a median of 0.37 (IQR 0.26 to 0.58, n = 14). The model single-family capacity is therefore above the real units on this measure.

Caution: the real-side median is below one, i.e. the proxy (hourly electricity x model COP) attributes more heat to the coldest hours than the units are rated for. That happens when the peak hour includes hot water or an electric back-up/boost heater (COP near one) or when the model COP overstates the cold-hour COP, so this real-side measure is an upper bound on delivered heat and the real thermal oversizing is not reliably measured. The firmer comparison is the nameplate one: SFH-low, model capacity / real rated capacity at A2/W35 = 1.53.

Direction of the effect if the cap were ever changed to match (no change is made): a smaller cap means less headroom, so fewer hours in which the thermostat and the optimiser can shift heat, likely lower savings and more hours at the cap; a larger cap means more headroom and the opposite. Peak heat and peak electricity would move with the cap wherever it binds (see the cap check in `heat_peaks.md`).

Limits of this check: the derived peak demand is a single hour and can fall in an hour with a high COP; n is small, most COPs are estimates, the real-side heat uses the model COP (not measured heat), and the meter includes hot water and any electric back-up where present.
